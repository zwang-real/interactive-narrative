Philadelphia · Yelp Open Dataset · 2022
What actually decides whether a restaurant survives in Philadelphia, and why good reviews are not enough to keep one open.
By Susan Zhang & Zijing Wang
Introduction

On a side street in Philadelphia, a restaurant called Garces Trading Company appeared on Yelp in the winter of 2010. It held a four-star rating and nearly 900 reviews from people who called it a neighborhood favorite. By 2018 it was gone. No announcement, no explanation, just a listing that stopped updating.

This is not a story about one restaurant. It is a story about what survival actually depends on, and how often it has nothing to do with how good a place is. Using Yelp's open dataset of more than seven thousand food and restaurant businesses in Philadelphia, we look for the things that separate the restaurants that lasted from the ones that closed. The pattern that emerges is structural, persistent, and largely invisible to the people living inside it. The most counterintuitive part is this: the restaurants that close are often not the worse ones.

The City's Pulse

Before we judge any single restaurant, it helps to pull the camera back. A city is never an even canvas. Where a restaurant opens, what it is able to offer, and what it costs to stay there shift from one block to the next. If the ground itself is uneven, then the question of who survives may be partly decided before a single door opens.

Where They Stand

This map plots every restaurant in our Philadelphia dataset, with color separating independents from chains and shading showing survival. At a glance it looks like scattered dots, but once you notice how the colors cluster, the structure of the city begins to surface.

Independent restaurants concentrate heavily in a handful of neighborhoods, while chains spread more evenly across the whole map. Some areas are almost entirely made of thriving independents, others are quietly dominated by chains. Location itself is a form of inequality. Where you open says something about how long you are likely to last. But place is only where the story begins.

More Reviews, Still Closing

If location shapes the starting line, what about quality? Intuition says the better-rated and more-loved a restaurant is, the longer it should last. This heatmap bins every restaurant by review count and rating, and splits them into two panels: the ones still open, and the ones that closed. If popularity protected restaurants, the closed panel should be nearly empty in the high-rating, high-review cells.

It is not. The closed panel stays crowded exactly where we would expect safety, among restaurants with strong ratings and hundreds of reviews. A high score and a loyal following do not move a restaurant out of danger. Independents do survive at lower rates than chains, 53.3% against 70.4%, but that gap sits to the side of the larger point. Customer love does not convert into survival. The question is no longer how good a restaurant is. It is what actually decides who stays.

What Determines Survival

The answer is not in the reviews. It lives in the choices a business makes before a single customer walks through the door: whether it offers delivery, how it prices itself, what kind of neighborhood it sits in. These decisions are quiet and easy to overlook, yet they shape the ending.

Does Any of It Help?

This is an interactive dashboard. Click through the variables on the left, delivery, price, cuisine, parking, and the survival rate on the right shifts with your choice. We invite you to find out for yourself which decisions actually move the odds and which barely matter at all.

The strongest signal by far is delivery. Restaurants that offer it survive at 68.9%, compared to just 33.6% for those that do not, a gap of nearly thirty-five points. Cuisine matters too, ranging from Caribbean at 71.6% down to French at 31.4%. Parking and takeout, by contrast, make almost no difference. What separates survivors is less about how good the food is and more about structural choices that decide how many customers a business can reach.

Place Matters

If operational choices matter, what about the economic ground a restaurant stands on? These two maps place neighborhood rent on the left beside restaurant ratings on the right, year by year. We want to see whether survival is shaped not only by the restaurant itself but by the cost of the land beneath it.

The pattern is uneven. Higher-rent and faster-rising neighborhoods tend to be harder places for independent restaurants to hold on. The same well-reviewed shop that might survive in an affordable area can struggle in a high-rent one. Location is not just a coordinate, it is a recurring bill. Structural disadvantage stacks, layer by layer, on the independents that were already the most exposed.

It Did Everything Right

The data has shown us the patterns. Now, carrying those patterns with us, we return to the restaurant we started with and grade its report card, line by line. You decide whether each measure beats the city.

The Report Card

Garces Trading Company offered delivery when only 54.5% of restaurants did. It held 4.0 stars against a city average of 3.58. It drew roughly 900 reviews when the median restaurant had thirty-three. It had parking, like 65.3% of the city, and sat at the most common price tier. Grade it yourself, see if every box turns green.

By every measure we have seen, it stood on the side of the survivors. And yet it closed in 2018. The data can tell us the truth at the level of the group, that independents are systematically disadvantaged in ways that have nothing to do with how good the food is. But the fate of a single restaurant still answers to forces the data cannot see: a lease that ends, a decision made upstairs, a shock no one planned for. Structural inequality is real. It is just not the whole story. The stars do not save you. They only let us see that the game was uneven from the start.
